## Abstract

Autonomous coding agents can run complex biological analyses from a single prompt, yet running them on personal machines threatens scientific reliability. In local shells, agents invent conflicting pipelines, write disposable scripts, hit hardware limits, and leave no verifiable trail. Here we build something that has never existed: a shared public plane where agents conduct data analysis responsibly at scale. By connecting any language-model agent—or Orbit, our dedicated research workbench—to public Galaxy cyberinfrastructure via community skills, the Model Context Protocol, Workflow Foundry, and BRC-Analytics, we route compute-intensive steps onto national supercomputers and record the complete lineage in a shareable research notebook. We evaluate this architecture across 47 published benchmark datasets (46 distinct biological cohorts) and one novel structural phylogenomics study spanning spatial transcriptomics, pathogen and vector functional genomics, and epidemiology. Complete workflow provenance uncovers unstated statistical pooling and post-processing rules in published fungal and mosquito studies alongside an undocumented 1,000-cell tutorial subset choice in a recent *Nature* Paper2Agent spatial transcriptomics benchmark. Beyond auditing past work, the platform enables cutting-edge discovery at scale. It collapses 146 fragmented *Plasmodium vivax* antigen subfamilies into 73–76 structural classes across local CPU and remote NVIDIA A100 GPU runs—with 21 three-continent classes holding over 97% of the repertoire—and dates 42 pathogen epidemic alignments in seconds of core manifold computation per cohort rather than hours to days of Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling. Our system unites the speed of autonomous agents with strict reproducibility, showing what future science can achieve on shared public infrastructure.

# Introduction

Across all scientific domains, LLM-powered agents formulate hypotheses, build analytical pipelines, execute code, and draft complete research manuscripts [1–6]. Recognizing these capabilities, academic and corporate laboratories have created purpose-built research environments such as Google's AI Co-Scientist to coordinate autonomous research [7]. Yet domain platforms cannot match the rapid development velocity, model optimizations, and tooling ecosystems powering general-purpose agent harnesses from the major artificial intelligence providers—Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI. At our own research institutions, we have watched numerous graduate students, postdocs, and PIs (even those who are AI-averse) adopt these agentic tools, become hooked, and stop analyzing data in any other way. This quickly makes general agent harnesses the primary working environment for modern computational research.

This rapidly widening use of agentic tools in science is equally thrilling and terrifying. The thrill comes from the speed and seemingly unlimited number of possibilities that suddenly became available to any researcher. The terror comes from a completely new set of scientific challenges that are not immediately apparent but will become severe as agentic science becomes the new norm. First, provenance tracking is largely absent. As unsupervised agents iterate, they generate ephemeral shell scripts, unversioned intermediate tables, and temporary scratch files. These ad-hoc artifacts vanish as soon as the session closes, and few have the proper version control skills and discipline to keep the full record. Scientific research requires that every analytical operation, tool version, and parameter choice be formalized into an immutable, shareable record so that colleagues can inspect, verify, and reproduce the complete computational trajectory. Second, autonomous agents require community-curated skills and standardized, high-quality bioinformatics tools. Rather than improvising custom scripts from unvetted online snippets, agents must make analytical decisions guided by verified computational protocols and validated tool specifications that function across different agent harnesses. Third, the agentic revolution does not change the need for infrastructure access. While modern laptops handle exploratory scripting effectively, production biological workflows—such as large-scale genome assembly, analyses of large multi-sample datasets, or deep learning inference—demand high-performance computing clusters with hundreds of processor cores, massive memory allocations, and dedicated hardware accelerators. Installing autonomous agent harnesses directly onto shared institutional supercomputers violates facility security policies and remains administratively unsustainable.

For over twenty years, the Galaxy platform has provided open, reproducible cyberinfrastructure to life scientists worldwide [8]. It now serves as an ideal agentic plane to address these challenges. First, it provides a universal compute abstraction that schedules complex, containerized analytical workloads across heterogeneous institutional clusters, national supercomputing allocations, and commercial clouds without requiring interactive login credentials, private keys, or command-line administrative access. Second, Galaxy's web interface and history system serve as an ideal artifact display mechanism. Every analytical step, parameter choice, and intermediate dataset is recorded within an immutable directed acyclic graph that investigators and their collaborators can inspect, audit, visualize, and re-execute with a single click. Third, the platform supplies the definitive scientific knowledge base. The Intergalactic Utilities Commission maintains thousands of vetted, containerized tool wrappers [9, 10], the Intergalactic Workflow Commission curates standardized analytical pipelines [11], and the Galaxy Training Network provides hundreds of peer-reviewed, step-by-step procedural tutorials [12, 13]. Finally, the Workflow Foundry translates external bioinformatics knowledge—including Nextflow pipelines and published methods—into structured agentic skills that guide autonomous execution across Galaxy cyberinfrastructure [14]. Galaxy grounds autonomous language models in transparent, reproducible, and vendor-agnostic scientific infrastructure.

Here we demonstrate our platform across seven biological investigations encompassing 48 empirical evaluations. These reproduce 47 published datasets (46 distinct cohorts: 5 transcriptomic, epigenomic, single-cell, and spatial studies plus 42 pathogen alignments across 41 outbreaks from 37 publications) and conduct a novel three-genome structural phylogenomics analysis of malaria antigens. In spatial transcriptomics, Orbit packages a published conformal-prediction pipeline as User-Defined Tools on UseGalaxy.org, matches local point predictions to floating-point precision ($r = 1.0000$, $\Delta_{\max} < 10^{-15}$) on the 1,000-cell tutorial subset, and scales the workflow to the full 5,613-cell reference cohort to triple prediction-to-measurement correlation ($r = 0.0861$ to $0.2553$). Across bacterial, fungal, and mosquito vector genomics, autonomous workflows compiled from published methods and community skills reproduce multi-sample transcriptomic, ATAC-seq, and single-cell atlases while uncovering unstated statistical pooling, annotation mismatches, and post-processing rules. In structural phylogenomics, the agent builds unprivileged User-Defined Tools to execute deep-learning protein language models across local and remote GPU/CPU resources, collapsing 146 fragmented *Plasmodium vivax* antigen subfamilies into 73 (local CPU reference) to 76 (remote A100 GPU and 32-core CPU) structural classes, with 21 three-continent classes holding over 97% of the repertoire. In genomic epidemiology, the platform automates emergence dating across 42 published pathogen epidemic alignments, evaluating tree-free molecular clock estimates against published Bayesian benchmarks in seconds per alignment. Across all seven investigations, every analytical step, containerized tool, and intermediate dataset is documented as an immutable history on public cyberinfrastructure.

# Results

## Advanced models propose vastly different analysis plans ...

Today many biological researchers discover how seemingly easy it is to conduct data analysis with agents. Suppose a student is given a task of analyzing RNA-seq data by an advisor. The student is adventurous enough to try to use one of the widely advertised agents. The student uploads data, explains to the agent the experimental design, and asks it to propose an analysis plan (note that planning is not a widely adopted discipline in biomedical research and that appropriate use of agents requires significant changes to the present curriculum). How does the student know that the proposed plan is good? How would the student choose among possibilities proposed by an agent? To illustrate this problem we asked 30 agents (10 for each of Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, respectively) to propose reanalysis plans for a paper (because we know what the expected outcome should be) describing a published transcriptomic study (PMC12804731) [15]. The 30 plans fragmented into 18 distinct eight-step plans (Figure [fig:agent_trajectories]). Even when restricted to the five result-changing biological and statistical steps (reference assembly, read aligner, quantified genomic feature, ribosomal RNA handling, and differential expression model scope), the plans formed 11 distinct five-step plans. The most common five-step plan was shared by only 7 of the 30 plans, meaning 23 of 30 diverged from the most common plan and 29 of 30 diverged from the published study's exact workflow. These conflicting choices across reference genomes, read strandedness, and statistical sample pooling also result in dramatic changes in resource requirements, as shown in Supplementary Table [tab:agent_resources].

## ... and leave a dusty trail of intermediates

To test how autonomous agents perform in practice, we tasked one agent from each model family with executing an end-to-end reanalysis on dedicated local hardware (Supplementary Table [tab:agent_resources]). During live execution, each agent deviated further from its model family's most common plan as it adapted to local tool availability and archive metadata. Even so, all three recovered the expected biological signals (Figure [fig:agent_execution]). Each confirmed glucose repression of the enterotoxin subunit *nheB*, induction of enterotoxin *entFM*, and genomic absence of the *hbl* operon. Yet beneath this biological concordance lay total computational divergence. The three platforms selected incompatible reference assemblies, different aligners, and conflicting statistical models. Their resource footprints varied by orders of magnitude (Supplementary Table [tab:agent_resources]). To bridge missing infrastructure, the agents generated twenty-six distinct custom scripts totaling over 1,200 lines of unversioned code (Supplementary Table [tab:agent_scripts]). Their engineering strategies differed fundamentally. Gemini executed three monolithic shell and R scripts with hardcoded local paths. Claude built nine wrapper scripts to drive containerized Nextflow pipelines. Codex engineered fourteen modular scripts to enforce memory limits and track pruned files. None of these custom scripts were portable or auditable beyond the machines where they ran. Each agent left behind an ephemeral debris field of temporary glue code, isolated environments, and unversioned logs. Autonomous agents can recover biological signals. They cannot produce shareable, permanent scientific records without a common cyberinfrastructure plane.

## Agentic science on public infrastructure: The Galactic plane

The benchmarking results above highlight the core problems of unassisted agentic science: conflicting and inconsistent analytical trajectories, unversioned glue code, and the limits of local hardware (Figures [fig:agent_trajectories] and [fig:agent_execution]). To produce trustworthy and verifiable results, agents need field-specific guidance and shared infrastructure (Figure [fig:architecture]). Domain skills and the Model Context Protocol (MCP) establish essential guardrails (Figure [fig:architecture], Panel 2) [16, 17] by following community-curated analysis pathways and preferring validated tool invocations to generating transient code. But agents also need a uniform, shared execution engine for three reasons. First, compute scale and access: a modern laptop can process large amounts of data, but memory-intensive aligners like STAR [18] quickly tax local memory and storage when run serially across multi-sample cohorts on a personal machine. Making agentic science accessible also requires accounting for researchers who lack powerful local hardware. Second, tools impossible to run locally: methods like, for example, AlphaGenome [19], Logan [20], or LexicMap [21] require massive indices or large models that no workstation can host. Third, and this is critical: reproducibility. One can argue that agentic outputs are reproducible because models can summarize their own actions. In practice, this requires strict discipline—such as running every analysis from versioned Git repositories—that few biologists maintain. A viable system must track computational provenance automatically without relying on user habits. Agents can prototype locally, but the final outcome must be pushed and executed on the cloud, detaching the analysis from irreproducible local silos. Pushing computation to public infrastructure guarantees provenance by default (Figure [fig:architecture], bottom loop). Every input, tool version, custom script, metadata, and intermediate dataset becomes part of a permanent record that can be examined and shared.

![figures/flow.pdf](figures/flow.pdf)

***The flow of verifiable agentic science: from Agent through Infrastructure to Result.** 
**1**, Agent Layer: The architecture supports two modes of agentic use. Researchers can connect any generic agent (such as Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, or Antigravity CLI) via community skills and MCP, or run Orbit, an opinionated Pi.dev desktop agent that adds Galaxy agent harness logic, notebook export, and support for commercial, public API, and local models. 
**2**, Guardrails: Domain skills supply procedural bioinformatics intelligence, validating parameters and tool suitability before execution. All analytical operations, execution logs, and output datasets stream back into a living research notebook, preserving an immutable directed acyclic graph with permanent computational provenance.*

The Galaxy ecosystem provides this execution engine (Figure [fig:architecture], Panel 3) [8]. Backed by twenty years of tool execution expertise, its vast library of tutorials and workflows supplies procedural skills for diverse analyses. Galaxy schedules any workload complexity—embarrassingly parallel tasks, large shared-memory assemblies, and GPU models—across public cyberinfrastructure. Researchers never have to negotiate agentic shell or head-node access with high-performance computing administrators—the execution fabric is already there.

Our vision for agentic science unites any agent runtime (Figure [fig:architecture], Panel 1), community-curated skills—drawn from tutorials, workflows, and purpose-built skills—and public infrastructure. Agents reuse existing tools first. When a missing step requires an ad-hoc script, such as a converter for an unconventional format, the agent writes and registers a tool directly into the workflow, where it can later mature into permanent software. Across thousands of users and hundreds of thousands of analyses, recurring patterns surface to form a community cloud of such ad-hoc tools.

## Reproducing a paper using a generic agent on the Galactic plane

To test this architecture in practice, we tasked an autonomous agent with reproducing the *Bacillus cereus* RNA-seq benchmark (PRJEB86181) from the earlier section, connecting it directly to UseGalaxy.org through the Galaxy Model Context Protocol (MCP) server [15, 16]. Unlike unconstrained local agents that generated dozens of unversioned scripts or stalled from disk exhaustion, this execution ran entirely on remote supercomputing allocations (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]). As we communicated with the agent and inspected its proposed workflows in Galaxy, we noticed that it initially selected HISAT2, a eukaryotic spliced aligner. Because *Bacillus cereus* is an intronless bacterium, spliced alignment adds computational overhead without biological benefit, so we directed the agent to switch to Kallisto pseudoalignment against the 5,472 unique coding loci of the sequenced isolate *B. cereus* ATCC 10987 [22]. This switch reduced per-sample quantification runtime to 38–60 seconds per library (compared with 3–5 minutes per library for HISAT2 alignment and coordinate sorting on standard Galaxy workers, and 4.0–4.8 hours when HISAT2 jobs encountered worker contention) without generating multi-gigabyte coordinate-sorted BAM files, while eliminating spurious bacterial splice junctions. We spotted this mismatch because we knew what to look for, whereas a less experienced user might not. The broader strategy here is that as more researchers use this platform, our community skills improve alongside the underlying models—encoding the knowledge that bacterial RNA-seq requires different tools than eukaryotic RNA-seq.

Building a working pipeline also requires catching incompatibilities between tools and quirks in reference annotations. When assembling the workflow in Galaxy, the agent initially placed a standalone `tximport` step between Kallisto's output collection and DESeq2, which caused a single-table-to-collection type mismatch in the workflow editor before any jobs were launched (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]A). The agent fixed the workflow by removing the standalone `tximport` step and passing a transcript-to-gene lookup table (`tx2gene.tsv`) directly to DESeq2's built-in `tximport` aggregation mode (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]B, see Methods). By testing the workflow on a small subset of reads first, the agent also caught and merged duplicate coding sequence headers caused by bacterial ribosomal frameshifts before running all five libraries (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]C, see Methods).

The resulting analysis reproduced the published biological findings closely (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]D, see Methods). Glucose supplementation repressed the primary lytic enterotoxin subunit *nheB* fivefold ($p_{\text{adj}} = 2.99 \times 10^{-3}$) and *nheA* nearly threefold, induced cell-wall enterotoxin *entFM* nearly threefold, and left regulatory subunit *nheC* unchanged. Across the 5,250 expressed coding loci (of 5,472 unique coding loci in ATCC 10987), Kallisto pseudoalignment and HISAT2 spliced alignment showed near-perfect agreement ($r = 0.9622$, $R^2 = 0.9257$). Every dataset, tool parameter, and analytical step is recorded on UseGalaxy.org in a workflow, history, and interactive research notebook (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]C, Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]). The agent left behind an executable scientific asset instead of ephemeral glue code.

## An opinionated agent

Skills and MCP guide generic agents well, as the *Bacillus cereus* replication above shows. For new users in particular—especially biologists who have never run command-line agents from the shell—there is a clear need for a friendly, lightweight desktop agent with additional agent harness logic. That agent harness logic matters because it gives tighter control over skills and MCP endpoints, adding extra guardrails to guide the analysis. We therefore developed Orbit, a lightweight desktop application built on top of Pi.dev (Figure [fig:architecture], Panel 1).

One of Orbit's advantages is that it provides Galaxy-specific features, including direct research notebook export, live job tracking, and unprivileged User-Defined Tool registration [23]. It also supports multiple language models: commercial models via API keys or subscriptions, public models via API keys, and local models running directly on the machine where Orbit runs. Thus, we support two modes of agentic use on the Galactic plane (Figure [fig:architecture], Panel 1): researchers can connect any generic agent via community skills and the Galaxy MCP server, or they can run our opinionated agent, Orbit, which layers Galaxy-specific agent harness logic and multi-model flexibility on top of those same interfaces. Below, we evaluate Orbit across a wide range of biological applications—spanning spatial transcriptomics, fungal and vector transcriptomics, ATAC-seq, single-cell atlases, structure-aware phylogenomics, and pathogen emergence dating—producing reproducible, provenance-rich records for every analysis (Table [tab:validation_summary]).

## From papers to skills: A spatial transcriptomics example

Recently, Miao et al. [24] benchmarked their Paper2Agent system by predicting spatial expression and uncertainty for *Acta2* across 3,405 mouse somatosensory cortex cells. We selected this study as the first demonstration of Orbit's functionality. While the idea of converting papers to agents is sound, we wanted to go even further—package a paper's analytical method into reusable tools and workflows on public infrastructure that any agent can invoke.

Single-cell spatial transcriptomics maps gene expression within tissue architecture, and algorithms such as TISSUE [25] estimate calibrated conformal prediction intervals for unmeasured genes from paired single-cell RNA-seq references. While the Paper2Agent Extended Data Figure 1c benchmark replicated the TISSUE GitHub tutorial on a 1,000-cell reference subset, their Data Availability section cited only the full 5,613-cell Zenodo Dataset 15 archive (see Methods). Prompted to reconcile this documentation gap, Orbit registered two multi-core User-Defined Tools for TISSUE on UseGalaxy.org, reproduced the 1,000-cell tutorial benchmark, and scaled the workflow to the full 5,613-cell reference dataset.

Within the 33-gene 10-fold cross-validation workflow, *Acta2* correlation reached $r = 0.2845$ ($81.03\%$ coverage), though absolute single-gene spatial correlation remained modest ($R^2 = 0.065\text{–}0.081$). Calibrated coverage held across both *Acta2* ($85.61\%$) and 10-fold cross-validation of all 33 spatial panel genes (see Methods, Supplementary Table [tab:case1_artifacts]). Across this and all subsequent analyses below, computations ran on UseGalaxy.org and ACCESS-CI cyberinfrastructure, using Orbit as the agent workbench and BRC-Analytics for pathogen and vector dataset staging (Table [tab:validation_summary]).

## Uncovering analysis inconsistencies and defunct tools

To solidify these findings, we applied the strategy above to a much more complex multi-step analysis. Here we combined Orbit with two additional platform components: Workflow Foundry [14], a skill-compilation framework that translates published methods, Nextflow pipelines, and CWL workflows into statically validated Galaxy skills and workflows, and BRC-Analytics [36] (see Methods), which resolves curated reference genome assemblies and sequencing runs. We reconstructed the 30-sample RNA-seq study of Hokken et al. [26] on itraconazole-induced transcriptional plasticity in *Aspergillus fumigatus* (BioProject PRJNA482512, three clinical isolates across five timepoints in duplicate). Starting from the publication text, BRC-Analytics staged the *A. fumigatus* Af293 reference genome and all 30 sequencing runs, while Workflow Foundry compiled a 60-step Galaxy workflow combining read trimming, STAR alignment with gene counting, DESeq2 modeling, functional enrichment, cross-study set comparisons, and visualization (Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]). Across all 30 libraries, the mean unique STAR alignment rate reached 86.88% (matching the 86.5% reported in the paper) and strand-column ratios verified that the libraries were unstranded before downstream modeling (see Methods).

Reproducing the published differential expression counts uncovered an unstated statistical design detail (see Methods). Fitting four separate 12-sample pairwise DESeq2 models matched the published fold changes ($r \ge 0.999$) but missed up to 39% of differentially expressed genes ($\text{baseMean} \ge 30$, $p_{\text{adj}} < 0.1$, and $|\log_2\text{FC}| > 1$) at later timepoints because smaller sample sizes inflated standard errors. Inspecting the published supplementary table revealed a single identical `baseMean` value across all four contrasts for each gene, indicating that the authors fitted one pooled 30-sample model across all five timepoints rather than pairwise contrasts. Switching the workflow to a pooled 30-sample DESeq2 fit reproduced the published standard errors to four decimal places and matched the published gene counts within 0.3–1.3% at every timepoint ($r = 0.9996\text{–}0.9999$, Figure [fig:brc_reproductions]A). Because the study's original Gene Ontology web service (AspGD GO Term Finder) had been decommissioned and term redundancy reduction (REVIGO) operated only as an interactive browser utility, the agent built three User-Defined Tools to automate hypergeometric GO enrichment, cross-study Venn overlaps, and term redundancy reduction. Together, these tools reproduced both the temporal enrichment inversion and the drug-efflux transporter trajectories within a single Galaxy history (Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]).

## Uncovering undocumented analysis details

Complete workflow provenance also separates biological reproducibility from undocumented table post-processing. We reanalyzed the ATAC-seq study of Ruiz et al. [27], which profiled chromatin accessibility in *Anopheles gambiae* midguts and salivary glands across two *Plasmodium falciparum* infections (GEO GSE152924). Calling sub-nucleosomal peaks ($< 130$ bp) with the authors' cited MACS2 version reproduced the underlying biology without free parameters, recovering 90.7–93.2% of the authors' deposited genomic regions across all four libraries (see Methods). Reproducing the paper's headline region counts (43,010 midgut and 68,576 salivary-gland regions), however, required diagnosing how replicate peaks were tabulated. Whereas standard consensus merging yielded far fewer intervals, inspecting the published supplementary table showed that each replicate peak was retained as an unmerged row. Counting per-replicate rows under the stated 51% reciprocal-overlap rule closed almost the entire midgut gap ($41,580$ rows [$-3.3\%$] on MACS2 2.1.2 and $41,340$ [$-3.9\%$] on MACS2 2.1.1) and left a $-16.1\%$ to $-16.5\%$ residual in salivary glands ($57,518$ and $57,274$ rows). Applying a relaxed empirical near-containment threshold (`either_frac` $= 0.851$) closed the salivary-gland residual to $-1.4\%$ ($67,594$ rows) while overshooting midguts by $+4.1\%$ ($44,780$ rows, Figure [fig:brc_reproductions]B, see Methods). Archiving both the unfitted coordinate recovery and the post-processing exploration in public Galaxy histories and workflows (Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]) preserved a clear boundary between verifiable chromatin biology and table counting conventions.

## Dealing with disparate annotation formats

We next reanalyzed the adult female *Aedes aegypti* midgut single-cell RNA-seq atlas of Wang et al. [28], processing the AaegL5.0 reference assembly and raw 10x Genomics libraries through STARsolo, DropletUtils, Scanpy, and edgeR on UseGalaxy.org (see Methods). Running a non-model vector through standard single-cell quality control surfaced an immediate annotation mismatch: VectorBase mitochondrial genes carry standard `AAEL` locus tags rather than an `MT_` prefix, causing generic Scanpy pipelines to miscalculate mitochondrial read fractions until the agent prefixed those features in the annotation. Across the two biological replicates, droplet filtering and clustering retained 6,707 post-QC singlets, closely matching the 7,147 cells reported by Wang et al. Harmonizing the 5,953 cells in the three major conserved populations—enterocyte/digestive, hemocyte-like epithelial, and visceral muscle—and running sample-blocked pseudobulk differential expression in edgeR recovered the study's top published cell-type markers alongside canonical digestive enzymes ($\text{FDR} \le 0.002$, see Methods). The complete lineage from raw reads to clustered cell objects and differential expression tables is archived on UseGalaxy.org (Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]).

## Heavy-Weight Model Orchestration and Structure-Aware Phylogenomics

We then wanted to stress-test our ability to leverage powerful infrastructure in an analysis that would require substantial computation. The *Plasmodium vivax* *vir* multigene family encodes nearly 1,000 exported surface adhesins per genome (748 to 985 PF05795-positive VIR proteins across the assemblies analyzed here) that mediate antigenic variation [29, 37–41]. Rapid sequence divergence pushes many homologs into the twilight zone ($\ge 30\%$ identity floor in MMseqs2). As a result, standard sequence clustering across reference assemblies from Indonesia (PvP01, short-read Illumina with subtelomere scaffolding [38]), Thailand (PvW1, long-read [39]), and Peru (PvPAM, long-read [40]) splits the repertoire into 203 sequence clusters (146 subfamilies with $\ge 3$ members), which previously suggested continent-specific radiation. Yet the crystal structure of a related *Plasmodium* PIR adhesin reveals a conserved $\alpha$-helical core despite extreme loop divergence [30]. To test whether structure-aware phylogenomics unifies this repertoire across continents, Orbit registered ten User-Defined Tools on UseGalaxy.org (one GPU tool and nine multi-core CPU tools). Together, these tools package the 4.4-gigabyte ProstT5 protein language model, Foldseek 3Di clustering, FoldMason structural alignment, synteny analysis, and IQ-TREE 3 maximum-likelihood phylogenetics across ACCESS-CI NVIDIA A100 GPUs and multi-core CPUs (see Methods, Supplementary Table [tab:case2_artifacts]).

Combining agentic capability with the Galactic plane unites autonomous analytical power with public computing infrastructure and reproducibility by default, defining how agents should drive empirical science.

# Discussion

Autonomous agents are undeniably powerful, yet one of their greatest dangers is that they make complex data analysis seem easy. Any student, postdoc, or principal investigator without formal statistical or computational training can now prompt an agent, receive polished figures and tables within minutes, and feel like an instant expert. As our multi-agent benchmark and reanalyses show, however, effortless execution often masks flawed analytical choices: mismatched reference assemblies, unpooled variance estimates, reversed library strandedness, or undocumented subset and post-processing filters. Proper procedural guidelines in the form of curated skills, paired with infrastructure that tracks computational provenance automatically, are therefore becoming essential. The urgency of this problem may not be obvious to everyone yet because the coming avalanche of agent-generated studies has not fully hit the scientific literature—but it is on the way.

What we propose here is a practical solution to this challenge because it builds directly on community components that already exist and operate at scale. Rather than inventing a closed platform from scratch, our architecture connects agents to Galaxy public cyberinfrastructure, BioContainers [10], curated tool wrappers [9], standardized workflows [11], Galaxy Training Network tutorials [12, 13], Workflow Foundry [14], and BRC-Analytics [36]. We recognize that not every laboratory will adopt this specific stack. Even so, grounding autonomous agents in shared, provenance-tracking community infrastructure provides a working blueprint for keeping agentic science reliable and verifiable.

This approach also brings new challenges of its own. Unprivileged User-Defined Tools remove the administrative friction of installing custom software, which means users and agents can easily create thousands of ad-hoc tools that perform nearly identical tasks. Left unmanaged on isolated machines, such proliferation would only deepen software fragmentation. When mediated through centralized public infrastructure—such as the UseGalaxy instances in the United States, Europe, and Australia—that same proliferation becomes an opportunity. Because tool definitions and executions are recorded in a shared environment, the community can monitor and curate an emerging tool cloud. Recurring usage patterns across thousands of analyses reveal which ad-hoc wrappers solve common bottlenecks and should be consolidated, tested, and hardened into permanent community software.

Several limitations of the present study warrant emphasis. First, while our planning benchmark evaluated $n = 10$ independent runs per model family, our live execution comparisons and biological case studies represent $n = 1$ demonstration runs per platform and incorporated human investigator steering during interactive sessions. This steering included directing the switch from HISAT2 to Kallisto in the *B. cereus* Galaxy replication, prompting Orbit to reconcile the 1,000-cell tutorial subset against the 5,613-cell Dataset 15 reference in the spatial study, and iteratively debugging User-Defined Tool containers and parameters. Second, connecting agents to public infrastructure does not eliminate model-level execution failures. In a companion eight-model autonomous benchmark on *Candida auris* antifungal-resistance transcriptomics archived in our repository (`candida-auris-benchmark/`), six of eight model runs completed differential expression workflows on UseGalaxy.org while two failed or stalled (one from an expired token after 86 tool calls and one after staging inputs). Similarly, on the Galaxy branch of the *P. vivax* VIR study, FoldMason structural alignment retained 176 of 336 sub-cluster representatives after occupancy filtering compared with all 338 on the local CPU reference branch. In the 42-cohort ChronAeon batch run, 5 of 42 initial Galaxy container jobs hit a 1-minute `sed` timeout and required re-execution and collection merging. Third, the ChronAeon molecular-clock module was evaluated by its developers, and 24 of the 39 numeric reference intervals in the 42-cohort benchmark were reconstructed from prose approximations, clock rates, or Skygrid bounds rather than explicit published 95% highest posterior density intervals. Finally, executing unprivileged User-Defined Tools at community scale requires strict container sandboxing, resource quotas, and governance to prevent compute abuse and manage public supercomputing and commercial LLM API costs.

More broadly, it is no longer clear what scientific software itself will look like in the coming years. Projects such as Reverse Engineer Anything (REA) [46], which connect coding agents to decompilers and binary analysis tools to inspect and recreate existing programs from scratch, point toward a future where almost any software package is replaceable on demand. Instead of relying on stable, long-lived toolsets, computational biology may soon face constant reinvention of the same analytical methods. That shift makes a shared community view even more important. When newly generated or rewritten tools run on common public infrastructure, they can be shared openly, benchmarked side by side, and allowed to compete on real datasets. Transparent usage and provenance records then let the research community see which implementations actually perform best and emerge as the preferred standards for each domain.

## Multi-Agent Analytical Divergence Benchmark
We evaluated analytical divergence across autonomous agents using an RNA-seq study of carbohydrate-mediated enterotoxin repression in *Bacillus cereus* (BioProject PRJEB86181, PMC12804731) [15]. The primary contrast compared two glucose-supplemented replicates (ERR14660412 and ERR14660413) against three baseline tryptic soy broth control replicates (ERR14660420, ERR14660421, and ERR14660422) from nineteen RNA-seq libraries across seven growth conditions (six carbohydrate supplements plus tryptic soy broth control, alongside one whole-genome sequencing run, ERR14660405, totaling twenty ENA runs).

To quantify planning variation, we evaluated 30 isolated agents across three foundation model families: ten Claude Opus 5.5 agents (Anthropic Claude Code CLI, `cc`), ten Codex agents running GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI Codex CLI, `codex exec`), and ten Gemini 3.8 Flash agents within Antigravity (`agy`, Google). These version strings designate the frontier Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google CLI model snapshots evaluated in our early-2026 benchmark logs. Each agent received identical natural-language instructions in an empty local workspace directory with no prior session context, external memory, or access to Galaxy cyberinfrastructure (Table [tab:planning_prompt]). During planning, each agent operated under its native CLI tool permissions: web fetch/search and `curl` in Claude Code, live web search in a network-restricted shell sandbox in Codex, and URL reading plus shell execution in Antigravity. We parsed each generated plan across eight sequential analytical decisions: reference genome assembly, workflow engine, read trimming software, ribosomal RNA depletion strategy, read aligner, quantified genomic feature, differential expression model scope (19-sample pooled vs. 5-sample contrast-only), and log-fold-change shrinkage prior. Combinatorial trajectory diagrams were constructed from this discrete decision matrix. Model families exhibited distinct planning profiles shaped by both model priors and CLI tool permissions. Claude plans aligned to *Bacillus pacificus* anQ-h4 and pooled variance across all nineteen RNA-seq libraries. Codex plans aligned to the sequenced isolate *Bacillus cereus* ATCC 10987 and restricted the comparison to the five target replicates. Gemini plans showed severe instability across assemblies, citing a non-*Bacillus* assembly (`GCF_000008105.1`, *Salmonella enterica* serovar Choleraesuis SC-B67) mislabeled as *B. cereus* ATCC 10987 in one plan, citing the *B. cereus* ATCC 14579 chromosome accession (`AE016877.1`) for ATCC 10987 in another, and reversing library strandedness in four plans. Aligning against a wrong assembly or genus alters or invalidates gene coordinates and annotations. Inverting library strandedness discards or miscounts real transcripts. Pooling all nineteen libraries to estimate variance alters statistical power compared to analyzing only the five target replicates.

## Galaxy-Native Replication of *Bacillus cereus* RNA-seq
To evaluate interactive agent-assisted replication on public cyberinfrastructure, we connected the Antigravity CLI (`agy`) to UseGalaxy.org through the Galaxy Model Context Protocol server (`galaxy-mcp`) [16]. The five target RNA-seq libraries from BioProject PRJEB86181—two glucose-supplemented replicates (ERR14660412 and ERR14660413) and three tryptic soy broth control replicates (ERR14660420, ERR14660421, and ERR14660422)—were imported into a Galaxy history. We also staged the *Bacillus cereus* ATCC 10987 reference genome (`GCF_000008005.1` / `GCA_000008005.1`, BioProject `PRJNA74`) and RefSeq coding sequence transcriptome (4.59 Mb across 5,476 FASTA records mapping to 5,472 unique coding loci, Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]).

Before launching the full analysis, the agent validated its workflow on a 1,000-read pilot subset. In NCBI RefSeq bacterial CDS FASTA exports, genes containing programmed ribosomal frameshifts or internal stop codons are split across multiple coordinate intervals that share the same locus tag with numerical suffixes (`#1`, `#2`). Because Kallisto index construction rejects duplicate target identifiers, the agent deduplicated these split headers on the pilot subset before processing the full libraries.

After we directed the agent to replace its initial eukaryotic spliced aligner (HISAT2) with prokaryotic Kallisto pseudoalignment, the agent assembled and validated a reusable Galaxy workflow combining FastQC, fastp, Kallisto quant [22], DESeq2, and MultiQC (Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]). During workflow construction, Galaxy's workflow schema validator enforces strict input-output type compatibility across connected steps. When the agent initially wired a standalone `tximport` step between Kallisto's output collection of transcript-level abundance tables and DESeq2's collection-mode input, the editor flagged the single-table-to-collection mismatch in orange (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]A). Inspecting the validation response returned by the MCP server, the agent removed the standalone `tximport` step, generated a two-column transcript-to-gene mapping table (`tx2gene.tsv`), and configured DESeq2's integrated `tximport` mode to aggregate collection-level transcript abundances into gene-level counts (Figure [fig:bacillus_replication]B). Under glucose supplementation in this five-library single-contrast design ($n = 2$ glucose vs. $n = 3$ control replicates), primary lytic enterotoxin subunit *nheB* decreased 5.05-fold ($\log_2\text{FC} = -2.335$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 2.99 \times 10^{-3}$). Subunit *nheA* decreased 2.80-fold ($\log_2\text{FC} = -1.484$, raw $p = 0.0274$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.106$, HISAT2 $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.0917$), cell-wall enterotoxin *entFM* was induced 2.70-fold ($\log_2\text{FC} = +1.432$, raw $p = 0.0158$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.0730$, HISAT2 $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.0785$), and regulatory subunit *nheC* remained unaffected ($\log_2\text{FC} = -0.158$, raw $p = 0.843$, $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.907$). Both *nheA* and *entFM* reached nominal significance (raw $p < 0.05$) while falling just above the 5% false discovery rate threshold after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the 2,815 loci passing DESeq2 independent filtering. To verify that unspliced pseudoalignment against coding sequences matched whole-genome spliced alignment, we also ran a parallel HISAT2 and featureCounts pipeline against the full 5.43 Mb genome assembly. On UseGalaxy.org, Kallisto pseudoalignment completed in 38–60 seconds per library without generating intermediate BAM files, compared with 3–5 minutes per library for HISAT2 alignment and coordinate sorting on standard Galaxy workers ($\sim 5\times$ faster, and 4.0–4.8 hours when HISAT2 jobs encountered worker contention in history `bbd44e69cb8906b5d979c5818e07f6ad`). Across the 5,250 expressed bacterial coding loci with non-missing fold-change estimates (of 5,472 total coding loci), Kallisto pseudoalignment and HISAT2 spliced alignment produced near-identical $\log_2$ fold-change estimates (Pearson $r = 0.9622$, $R^2 = 0.9257$). Workflows, datasets, and comparison notebooks are archived on UseGalaxy.org (Supplementary Table [tab:brc_artifacts]).

## Galaxy Model Context Protocol and Modular Skills
Agents interact with computational infrastructure through the Galaxy Model Context Protocol server (`galaxy-mcp`) [16]. The Model Context Protocol establishes an open standard for tool execution and data inspection. Rather than allowing agents to execute arbitrary local shell commands, the server provides forty-four structured endpoints. These endpoints enable agents to search tool registries, inspect dataset collections, launch workflows, and monitor execution states through standard input-output streams or remote HTTP connections. The server is implemented in Python utilizing the official BioBlend library to communicate with the Galaxy REST API. Transport mechanisms include standard input-output (stdio) for local CLI integration and streamable HTTP for web and remote clients. Authentication occurs through long-lived Galaxy API keys or OAuth2 token exchanges supplied via environment variables (`GALAXY_API_KEY`). The server provides structured error handling and schema validation across all parameter endpoints.

Domain expertise is provided through modular skill packages [17]. These skills teach language models standard bioinformatics conventions. Skills guide tool discovery, parameter selection, and collection manipulation idioms. For complex multi-step pipelines, Workflow Foundry skills compile validated workflow templates directly into agent memory [14]. By separating operational skills from the underlying compute runtime, the framework ensures that agents execute verified analytical procedures rather than generating unvalidated guesses.

## User-Defined Tool Architecture
User-Defined Tools are written in YAML adhering to the `GalaxyUserTool` class definition. The tool wrapper specifies a mandatory container image hosted on public registries (Quay.io, Docker Hub), typed input parameters, declared output datasets, and an execution command. Commands are templated using sandboxed ECMAScript evaluation executed inside an isolated V8 runtime. Output files are claimed from the working directory through `from_work_dir` designations or dynamic dataset discovery patterns.

## Orbit Workbench and Execution Guard
Orbit is constructed as an Electron desktop application powered by a headless agent runtime built as an extension on Pi.dev. The runtime manages session context, system prompts, and tool registration. The execution guard intercepts tool calls via internal lifecycle hooks, classifying actions across model tiers, path security, and shell hazard. Local file writes are strictly confined to the workspace root using realpath resolution, and authentication secrets (provider and Galaxy API keys) are passed via environment variables and scrubbed from tool outputs and session logs (`activity.jsonl`) through pattern-based secret redaction. Remote Galaxy jobs are tracked through YAML invocation blocks embedded within `notebook.md`, which are updated via background polling and asynchronous event handlers.

## Workflow Foundry and BRC-Analytics
Galaxy Workflow Foundry (`@galaxy-foundry/gxwf-foundry`) [14] is a skill-compilation pipeline implemented in TypeScript and Node.js that converts reusable procedural templates (Molds) into agent skills for diverse agent runtimes such as Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity, and Orbit. Each Mold is a Markdown specification carrying Zod-validated YAML frontmatter that declares upstream handoff schemas, design patterns, reference manuals, and downstream output artifacts. Rather than prompting an agent to invent a multi-step workflow in a single pass, Foundry decomposes workflow synthesis into staged skills that pass structured JSON and Markdown handoffs. These stages extract a structured summary from a published paper, Nextflow pipeline, or CWL workflow, design the Galaxy interface and collection data-flow topology, resolve curated ToolShed wrappers or User-Defined Tools, and assemble a `gxformat2` workflow step by step. Synthesized workflows are validated statically using the `gxwf` validation engine, which verifies graph topology, step connectivity, and tool parameter schemas against official Galaxy XML definitions and Intergalactic Workflow Commission (IWC) exemplars [11] before automated execution testing via Planemo.

BRC-Analytics (<https://brc-analytics.org>) [36] is the NIAID Bioinformatics Resource Center analytics portal built on public Galaxy infrastructure. It maintains a taxonomy-indexed catalog of curated pathogen, fungal, eukaryotic parasite, and vector reference assemblies, gene annotations, and public sequencing runs aggregated from NCBI Assembly, RefSeq, Ensembl, VEuPathDB, and the Sequence Read Archive (SRA/ENA). In autonomous workflows, BRC-Analytics removes the error-prone step of scraping FTP directories, reconciling assembly accession versions across databases, or downloading raw sequencing files to local storage. Agents query BRC-Analytics by taxonomic identifier or BioProject accession to resolve canonical reference FASTA sequences, GTF/GFF3 gene models, and paired or single-end SRA run collections and stage them directly into a target UseGalaxy.org history with preserved sample metadata.

## Tree-Free Manifold Emergence Dating Across 42 Pathogen Cohorts
Forty-two curated BEAST XML benchmark alignments (drawn from 37 primary publications across 41 distinct pathogen outbreaks plus a paired 14,517 bp coding tutorial partition, `41_skygrid_rabies_gill2020`, for the 2014 Sierra Leone Ebola cohort [33, 47]) were archived on UseGalaxy.org and parsed via UDT `chronaeon_batch_preparator` v1.0.0 into synchronized 42-element BEAST XML, aligned FASTA, and tip-date collections (Supplementary Table [tab:case3_artifacts]). Concurrent tree-free emergence dating was executed across all 42 alignments using UDT `chronaeon_cohort_analysis` v3.2.2 [35], completing in seconds of core manifold computation per alignment (for example, 2.58 seconds for TN93 AutoClock manifold regression in pilot benchmarking, compared with $5 \times 10^6$ to $10^9$ MCMC states taking 8–36 hours in dual-core BEAST X or 5–7 days in BEAST 2.7.7). During the initial 42-element batch execution on UseGalaxy.org, 5 of 42 container jobs hit a 1-minute `sed` timeout, were re-executed across recovery runs, and were merged into the final 42-element output collections via `__BUILD_LIST__`. For each alignment, the tool computed pairwise evolutionary distances, continuous manifold embeddings, analytical Fieller 95% confidence intervals [48], leave-one-out cross-validation error, AutoClock normalized Laplacian spectral bisection ($K^*$), and optional HyphAeon Attention PGLS root inference using offline-staged `model.viral.pt` weights.

To audit reference comparator provenance across all 42 alignments (`chronaeon_42cohorts_provenance.tsv` and Supplementary Table [tab:chronaeon_comparators]), we classified each cohort's reference interval into three categories. First, 15 alignments had explicit 95% highest posterior density (HPD) or Bayesian credible intervals (BCI) reported directly in the primary publication's text or tables (plus `39_chikv_reunion_dellicour2020` [49], which carries a published BEAST root of $2004.8$ [$2004.5, 2005.1$] in `published_comparators.tsv` but was marked `NO_COMPARATOR` in the automated runner). Second, 24 alignments had numeric reference intervals in `aggregate_benchmark_results.tsv` reconstructed from prose approximations (such as Hill and Baele's "approximately March 2014" TempEst intercept [33] bracketed as $2014.20$ [$2014.10, 2014.30$], or O'Toole et al.'s late-2015 APOBEC3 emergence estimate [50]), molecular clock calibrations, or Skygrid grid cutoffs. Third, 2 additional alignments were unscored (`NO_COMPARATOR`) in the automated runner and carried prose clade ranges in `published_comparators.tsv`: `38_mab_commins2023` (*Mycobacterium abscessus* [51], prose range $\sim 1960\text{–}1980$ vs. ChronAeon $1975.87$ [$1966.21, 1982.46$]) and `40_hiv1_crf01ae_philippines2024` (HIV-1 CRF01_AE Philippines, prose range $\sim 1995\text{–}2002$ vs. ChronAeon $1975.84$ [$1969.29, 1980.93$], alongside `39_chikv_reunion_dellicour2020` where ChronAeon inferred $1954.99$ [$1948.09, 1960.62$]). In addition, cohort `41_skygrid_rabies_gill2020` (14,517 bp Ebola coding partition) duplicated the summary row of cohort `00_ebola_sierraleone_gire2014` (4,453 bp variable-site partition) in `aggregate_benchmark_results.tsv`, whereas the single-pass rerun in `deterministic_benchmark_results.tsv` recorded its distinct 14,517 bp estimates ($2012.71$ OLS and $2002.60$ spline). We therefore report benchmark concordance and median errors both across all 42 rows (39 numeric comparators) and across the 41 unique alignments (38 unique numeric comparators).

On the 196-taxon 2014 Sierra Leone Ebola benchmark (cohorts `00` and `41` [31, 33]), the reconstructed tutorial origin is $2014.20$ [$2014.10, 2014.30$] and the Gire et al. 81-sequence published BEAST root is $2014.14$ [$2014.07, 2014.20$]. Galaxy TN93 OLS estimated a root of $2014.23$ (95% Fieller CI: [$2014.17, 2014.27$], cross-validation MAE: $26.4$ days, portal panel: $2014.30$ [$2014.24, 2014.34$], cubic spline: $2004.92$). For SARS-CoV-2 Gamma (P.1) [34], TN93 OLS inferred $2020.71$ (95% Fieller CI: [$2020.63, 2020.76$], MAE: $17.0$ days, portal PGLS panel: $2020.61$ [$1595.27, 2020.79$]) against the published BEAST root of $2020.87$ ([$2020.78, 2020.93$]). Because the OLS Fieller interval lies just below the published 95% HPD without overlapping, it qualifies as concordant by point-estimate proximity ($|\Delta t_{\mathrm{MRCA}}| = 0.16 < 1.0$ year). On the single-pass portal baseline plotted in Figure [fig:chronaeon_42cohorts], 18 of 39 numeric-comparator cohorts (46.2%, or 42.9% of 42) were visually concordant (interval overlap or $|\Delta t_{\mathrm{MRCA}}| < 1.0$ year) and 21 (50.0% of 42) diverged. ### Table S1: Resource allocation, software environments, and compute footprints across three autonomous agent execution runs.
*All three runs re-analyzed the primary glucose versus control contrast from the Bacillus cereus transcriptomic benchmark (BioProject PRJEB86181) on an identical host machine (Ubuntu Linux workstation with 24 CPU cores, 62 GB RAM, and NVMe SSD storage). While all three platforms recovered the core biological signal of enterotoxin regulation, their resource management paradigms differed sharply. Gemini utilized a lightweight, greedy Unix shell pipeline with immediate file cleanup. Claude executed a heavy containerized Nextflow workflow that consumed over 100 GB of scratch disk and caused disk exhaustion until secondary quality control tools were disabled. Codex applied strict computational budgeting. It capped memory buffers and thread allocations per stream while maintaining an explicit ledger of purged intermediate files. None of the three runs produced persistent provenance records that external researchers could inspect or re-execute without rebuilding the local execution environment.*
